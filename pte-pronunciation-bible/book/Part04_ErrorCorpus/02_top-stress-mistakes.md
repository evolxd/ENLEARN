# 2. Top Stress Mistakes（重音高频错误）

Part: Part IV — Chinese Error Corpus
Status: Draft
Accent model: General American (GA), consistent with Part II
L1 scope: Mandarin Chinese (普通话)

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Explain why Mandarin's tonal system, rather than a stress system, predicts specific English stress errors.
- Identify the difference between using pitch alone versus using the full stress bundle (pitch, length, loudness, vowel quality) to mark a stressed syllable.
- Recognize "equal stress" and "wrong-syllable stress" as the two dominant error types documented in the research literature.
- Apply targeted correction techniques distinct from those used for the segmental errors in Ch.1.

## Theory

### Key Terms 关键术语

This chapter reuses stress terminology defined in Part II Ch.4 (Word Stress) — primary stress, secondary stress, lexical stress, stress shift — rather than redefining it. New terms specific to this chapter:

| English | 中文 | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Tone (lexical) | 声调 | In Mandarin, a pitch contour that distinguishes word meaning — a different mechanism from English stress (Part II Ch.4). |
| Equal Stress Error | 平均重音错误 | Producing all syllables of a word with similar prominence instead of one clearly stressed syllable. |
| Stress-as-Tone Transfer | 声调化重音迁移 | Using pitch alone (in a tone-like way) to mark stress, rather than the full native stress bundle. |

### Why Mandarin's tone system predicts specific stress errors

Mandarin is a tonal language: pitch contour on each syllable is itself a phonemic feature that distinguishes word meaning, and Mandarin does not have an English-style system where one syllable is stressed and others are unstressed within a word `[Linguistics]` (Part II Ch.4). Because Mandarin speakers learn to attend to pitch for lexical meaning rather than for marking prominence, research on Mandarin EFL learners identifies two recurring, related error types when they produce English stress.

### Mistake 1 — Equal stress ("failure to differentiate stressed and unstressed syllables")

Research on Mandarin EFL learners' acquisition of English word stress identifies "negative transfer of mother language, failure to be sensitive to vowel length, and overgeneralization of English word stress assignment rules" as central causes of stress errors `[Linguistics]`. Because Chinese tone assignment operates independently of a stressed/unstressed contrast, learners often produce each syllable of a multisyllabic word with comparable length and prominence, rather than concentrating stress on one syllable and reducing the others `[Linguistics]` — this is the same underlying mechanism introduced in Part II Ch.4 and Ch.5, now identified specifically in the research literature as a documented, not merely anecdotal, error pattern.

### Mistake 2 — Marking stress with pitch alone, in a tone-like way

Acoustic research comparing Chinese-dialect speakers' production of English lexical stress to native speakers found that Chinese-speaking participants relied heavily on pitch cues and used inflexible pitch patterns across different stress positions, "producing English stress as tone" — in one documented pattern, using a level high tone with unusually long duration to indicate a stressed syllable `[Linguistics]`. This differs from native English stress, which is marked by a *bundle* of cues together — pitch, length, loudness, and full (non-reduced) vowel quality (Part II Ch.4) — not by pitch in isolation. A learner who only raises pitch on the target syllable, without also lengthening it and keeping neighboring syllables reduced, produces a stress pattern that sounds tonal rather than stressed.

### Mistake 3 — Wrong-syllable stress from overgeneralized rules

Because English stress is only partially rule-governed (Part II Ch.4), learners sometimes overgeneralize a pattern learned from one word family to a different word that does not follow it `[Linguistics]`. This differs from Mistake 1 (even prominence across syllables) — here, one syllable is clearly stressed, but it is the wrong one, changing the word's identity (e.g., *record* the noun vs *record* the verb, Part II Ch.4) rather than just its naturalness.

## Exam Strategy

- When marking a new word's stress (Part III), check not only *which* syllable is stressed but *how* — plan to combine pitch, length, and full vowel quality on that syllable, and reduce the others (Part II Ch.7), rather than relying on pitch alone.
- For noun/verb stress pairs, treat correct stress as equivalent to a Content-level distinction (Part I Ch.4) — a stress error here is not merely an accent feature.
- Exaggerate the length and vowel-reduction contrast during drilling, not just the pitch contrast — Mistake 2 shows that pitch alone is not a sufficient substitute for the full native stress bundle.

## Exercises

1. Record a four-syllable word. Measure (by ear, or with any available audio tool) whether the stressed syllable is both longer and louder than the others, or only higher in pitch.
2. Take three noun/verb stress-pair words (e.g. *present*, *record*, *permit*) and drill both forms, checking that the unstressed syllables reduce toward schwa (Part II Ch.7) rather than keeping a full vowel.
3. Identify one word in your own vocabulary where you may be applying a stress pattern learned from a different, similar-looking word (Mistake 3), and verify the correct pattern against a dictionary.

## Summary

- Mandarin's tonal system does not include an English-style stressed/unstressed contrast, predicting two documented, distinct error types `[Linguistics]`.
- Equal stress (Mistake 1): syllables produced with similar prominence instead of one standing out.
- Stress-as-tone (Mistake 2): stress marked with pitch alone, without the accompanying length and vowel-quality cues native stress requires.
- Wrong-syllable stress (Mistake 3): a rule overgeneralized from a different word family, changing word identity.
- Correct stress is a scored Pronunciation criterion independent of accent (Part I Ch.1–2), so these errors carry real, documented scoring consequences.
