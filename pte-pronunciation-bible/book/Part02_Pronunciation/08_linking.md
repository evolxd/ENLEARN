# 8. Linking（连读）

Part: Part II — Pronunciation Foundations
Status: Draft
Accent model: General American (GA); GA's rhoticity changes how consonant-to-vowel linking works compared to RP (noted below).

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Define linking and explain why it produces smoother, more continuous-sounding speech.
- Apply consonant-to-vowel linking across word boundaries.
- Explain GA's rhotic linking pattern and how it differs from RP's non-rhotic "linking r".
- Apply vowel-to-vowel linking with intrusive glides /j/ and /w/.

## Theory

### Key Terms 关键术语

| English | 中文 | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Linking | 连读 | Pronouncing adjacent sounds across a word boundary as a continuous unit rather than with a break between words. |
| Liaison | 连读现象 | A general term (borrowed from French phonetics) for sounds bridging across word boundaries. |
| Linking /r/ | 连读 R | In non-rhotic accents, an /r/ pronounced only when the next word begins with a vowel. |
| Intrusive Glide | 过渡音（插入的滑音） | A /j/ or /w/ sound inserted between two adjacent vowels across a word boundary to ease the transition. |

### Why linking happens

Linking is a direct consequence of the same forces introduced in Ch.6: English speech flows as a continuous stream rather than as discrete, separately-articulated words, and adjacent sounds influence each other across word boundaries just as they do within a word `[Linguistics]`. Where a word ends in a consonant and the next begins with a vowel, that consonant is typically pronounced as though it opens the following syllable, rather than being separated by a pause.

### Consonant-to-vowel linking

When a word ends in a consonant and the next word begins with a vowel sound, the final consonant links smoothly into the following vowel `[Linguistics]`:

- *"turn it off"* → /tɜːr nɪ tɒf/ (the /n/ of *turn* and /t/ of *it* and *off* all link forward into the next vowel)
- *"an apple"* → /ə ˈnæpəl/

This is one of the most direct, learnable techniques for producing the "smooth rhythm and phrasing" the Oral Fluency band descriptors describe (Part I Ch.3) `[Pearson]`/`[Linguistics]`, because it removes the small breaks between words that make speech sound word-by-word rather than continuous.

### GA rhotic linking vs RP's "linking r"

RP is non-rhotic: word-final /r/ is normally silent unless the next word begins with a vowel, at which point it is pronounced — this is the classically taught "linking r" phenomenon in British English teaching materials `[Linguistics]`. GA, by contrast, is rhotic: word-final /r/ is pronounced in all positions, including before a consonant or a pause `[Linguistics]`. Because this book uses GA as its default accent model, word-final /r/ in GA simply links naturally into a following vowel as an ordinary case of consonant-to-vowel linking (e.g. *"far away"* → /fɑːr əˈweɪ/) rather than as a special rule that only applies before vowels. If you have studied "linking r" from British-English-focused materials, note that the underlying behavior in GA is different: the /r/ is always there, not inserted only before vowels.

### Vowel-to-vowel linking

When one word ends in a vowel and the next begins with a vowel, English speech typically inserts a brief transitional glide — /j/ or /w/ — to bridge the two vowels smoothly, rather than producing an abrupt glottal break between them `[Linguistics]`:

- *"I am"* → /aɪ **j**æm/ (the off-glide of the diphthong /aɪ/ naturally produces a /j/-like transition)
- *"go on"* → /goʊ **w**ɒn/ (the off-glide of /oʊ/ naturally produces a /w/-like transition)

Which glide appears is predictable from the preceding vowel: front-vowel endings (like /iː/, /aɪ/, /eɪ/) tend to produce /j/; back, rounded-vowel endings (like /uː/, /oʊ/, /aʊ/) tend to produce /w/ `[Linguistics]`.

## Examples

*"Turn it off and go away."*

Linked: /tɜːr nɪ tɒf ən ɡoʊ wəˈweɪ/ — note the consonant-to-vowel links (*turn it*, *it off*), the reduced weak form of *and* (Ch.7), and the intrusive /w/ bridging *go* and *away*.

## Exam Strategy

- Practice linking deliberately across word boundaries in Read Aloud passages before reading them aloud in the exam — mark likely linking points (consonant-final word followed by vowel-initial word) during preparation time.
- Do not insert a pause or glottal stop between words that should link — this breaks the "smooth rhythm and phrasing" that Oral Fluency rewards (Part I Ch.3) even if every individual word is pronounced correctly.
- If you were taught British "linking r," retrain the underlying rule for GA: the /r/ is pronounced everywhere, not only when linking before a vowel.

## Exercises

1. Take a Read Aloud practice sentence and mark every point where a consonant-final word is followed by a vowel-initial word. Practice linking across each marked boundary.
2. Practice the vowel-to-vowel pairs *"I am"*, *"go on"*, *"the end"* (this one uses a different linking type — a glottal-stop-avoiding /j/-like transition after "the"), noting which glide naturally bridges each pair.
3. Record a sentence twice: once pausing slightly between every word, once linking naturally. Compare both against the Band 5 Oral Fluency description from Part I Ch.3.

## Summary

- Linking pronounces adjacent sounds across word boundaries as a continuous unit, removing artificial breaks between words `[Linguistics]`.
- Consonant-to-vowel linking carries a final consonant forward into the next word's initial vowel.
- GA is rhotic (word-final /r/ always pronounced), unlike RP's non-rhotic "linking r," which only inserts /r/ before a following vowel — this book's GA default means linking /r/ works differently than in British-English-focused materials.
- Vowel-to-vowel sequences typically bridge with an intrusive /j/ or /w/ glide, predictable from the preceding vowel's quality.
- Linking is one of the most direct, trainable techniques for producing the smooth phrasing rewarded by PTE Oral Fluency scoring.
