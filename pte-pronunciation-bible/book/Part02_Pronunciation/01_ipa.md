# 1. IPA（国际音标）

Part: Part II — Pronunciation Foundations
Status: Draft
Accent model: General American (GA). This book uses GA as its default reference accent (per `STYLE_GUIDE.md` §5 and Part I Ch.2's conclusion that consistency, not accent choice, is what matters for training). Received Pronunciation (RP) forms are noted only where they differ in a way relevant to the point being made.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Explain what the IPA is and why English spelling cannot reliably indicate pronunciation.
- Read a basic IPA transcription for an English word, including stress and length marks.
- State which accent model this book uses and why consistency matters more than which accent is chosen.
- Use IPA transcriptions from a dictionary as a training tool instead of guessing pronunciation from spelling.

## Theory

### Key Terms 关键术语

| English | 中文 | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet) | 国际音标 | A standardized set of symbols, one per distinct speech sound, used to transcribe any language unambiguously. |
| Phoneme | 音位 | The smallest sound unit that can change a word's meaning. |
| Grapheme | 字形（书写符号） | A letter or letter combination used in spelling — not the same as a sound. |
| Transcription | 音标标注 | Writing a word's pronunciation using IPA symbols instead of ordinary spelling. |
| Accent Model | 口音参照系统 | The specific accent (e.g. GA or RP) used as the reference point for a transcription. |

### Why English needs a separate sound alphabet

The IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet) is a standardized set of symbols, maintained by the International Phonetic Association, in which each symbol represents one distinct sound, usable across any language `[Linguistics]`. English needs this because its spelling system does not reliably map to pronunciation `[Linguistics]`: the letter "a" represents different sounds in *cat*, *father*, and *about*; the same /f/ sound is spelled three different ways in *fish*, *photo*, and *laugh*. A learner who tries to "read the letters" of English the way Pinyin's letters map fairly consistently to Mandarin sounds will systematically mispredict pronunciation `[Linguistics]`/`[Practice]`. IPA solves this by giving every distinct sound (phoneme) its own symbol, independent of spelling.

### Reading a basic transcription

An IPA transcription is written between slashes, e.g. /kæt/ for *cat*. Three conventions recur throughout this book:

- **Stress mark** (ˈ): placed immediately before the stressed syllable, e.g. /kəmˈfɜːrtəbəl/ for *comfortable* — Part II Ch.4 covers stress in depth.
- **Length mark** (ː): placed after a vowel symbol to show it is a "long" vowel, e.g. /iː/ in *see* vs the short /ɪ/ in *sit* — Part II Ch.3 covers vowels in depth.
- **Schwa** (ə): the neutral, reduced vowel sound found in most unstressed syllables, e.g. the second syllable of *sofa* /ˈsoʊfə/ — Part II Ch.7 (Reduction) covers this in depth.

### One phoneme, one symbol — and why that matters

A phoneme is the smallest sound unit that can change a word's meaning: *pit* and *bit* differ only in their first phoneme, /p/ vs /b/, and that single difference changes the word `[Linguistics]`. IPA transcription makes phoneme boundaries explicit in a way spelling does not — which is the entire reason Part III (Lexicon) and Part IV (Chinese Error Corpus) use IPA rather than respelling systems ("kat" for *cat*) that are common in some textbooks. Respelling systems recycle English letters, which reintroduces the same ambiguity IPA exists to remove.

## Examples

| Word | Spelling-based guess (misleading) | IPA (accurate) |
|---|---|---|
| laugh | "l-a-u-g-h" suggests a /g/ or /h/ sound | /læf/ |
| photo | "ph" looks unrelated to /f/ | /ˈfoʊtoʊ/ |
| comb | "b" suggests a /b/ sound | /koʊm/ |
| island | "s" suggests an /s/ sound | /ˈaɪlənd/ |

## Exam Strategy

- When learning a new PTE-relevant word (Part III), look up its dictionary IPA transcription before attempting to say it — do not guess pronunciation from spelling, especially for words with irregular spelling-to-sound correspondence like those above.
- Use one consistent dictionary (this book cites Cambridge and Oxford Learner's Dictionaries) so you are not comparing transcriptions from different accent models.
- When you shadow a word (Part III's "Shadowing" field), check your own recorded pronunciation against its IPA transcription, phoneme by phoneme, rather than against your general impression of "did that sound right."

## Exercises

1. Write the IPA transcription (look it up if needed) for five words whose spelling you find unpredictable in English. Note which letters misled you before you checked.
2. Find one minimal pair (two words differing by exactly one phoneme) in your own vocabulary list and write both in IPA, marking the single differing symbol.
3. Compare Cambridge and Oxford's IPA transcription for the same word. If they differ, identify whether the difference is an accent-model difference (GA vs RP) or a genuine disagreement.

## Summary

- The IPA gives one symbol per phoneme, independent of spelling, because English spelling does not reliably indicate pronunciation `[Linguistics]`.
- Transcriptions use slashes, a stress mark (ˈ), and a length mark (ː) as core conventions used throughout this book.
- A phoneme is the smallest sound unit that can change word meaning; minimal pairs demonstrate this directly.
- This book defaults to General American (GA) as its accent model for consistency, not because GA is "more correct" than RP.
- Always verify pronunciation against a dictionary's IPA transcription rather than guessing from spelling.
