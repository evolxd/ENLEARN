# 2. Consonants（辅音）

Part: Part II — Pronunciation Foundations
Status: Draft
Accent model: General American (GA); the consonant inventory itself is shared with RP.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- List the 24 English consonant phonemes, organized by place, manner, and voicing.
- Identify which English consonants are absent from Mandarin's phoneme inventory.
- Explain why Mandarin's syllable structure (limited codas, no consonant clusters) predicts specific, systematic error patterns rather than random ones.
- Distinguish a phoneme-inventory mismatch (a linguistic fact) from a pronunciation "mistake" in the casual sense.

## Theory

### Key Terms 关键术语

| English | 中文 | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Place of Articulation | 发音部位 | Where in the mouth a consonant is produced (e.g. lips, teeth, alveolar ridge). |
| Manner of Articulation | 发音方式 | How the airflow is shaped (e.g. fully stopped, forced through a narrow gap). |
| Voicing | 浊音／清音（声带振动） | Whether the vocal folds vibrate during the sound (voiced) or not (voiceless). |
| Consonant Cluster | 辅音连缀 | Two or more consonants pronounced together with no vowel between them. |
| Coda | 音节尾辅音 | A consonant (or consonants) closing a syllable, after the vowel. |
| Epenthesis | 增音 | Inserting an extra sound (often a vowel) that is not in the target word. |

### The English consonant inventory

English (GA and RP alike) has 24 consonant phonemes, commonly organized by place, manner, and voicing `[Linguistics]`:

### What Mandarin's inventory does and does not include

Mandarin Chinese has its own, different consonant inventory and syllable structure `[Linguistics]`. Two mismatches are especially predictable sources of error for Mandarin L1 learners:

1. **Missing phonemes.** Mandarin has no dental fricatives — /θ/ and /ð/ (as in *think* and *this*) do not exist in Mandarin's sound system `[Linguistics]`. Because these sounds must be produced with the tongue between the teeth, and Mandarin has no equivalent, learners typically substitute the nearest sound they do have, often /s/ or /f/ for /θ/ and /z/ or /d/ for /ð/ `[Linguistics]`/`[Practice]`. This is a phoneme-inventory gap, not carelessness — a full mechanism-level treatment of this and related substitutions belongs to Part IV (Chinese Error Corpus), which this chapter sets up.
2. **Syllable structure (coda) restrictions.** Mandarin syllables can only end in a vowel, /n/, /ŋ/, or a glide — no other consonant, and no consonant clusters, can close a Mandarin syllable `[Linguistics]`. English freely allows single final consonants (*bag* /bæg/), and clusters of two or more (*asks* /æsks/, *sixths* /sɪksθs/). Because Mandarin phonology does not have a slot for these codas, learners frequently either delete the final consonant (*bag* → "ba") or insert an extra vowel after it (*bag* → "ba-guh") to make the syllable conform to a pattern Mandarin allows `[Linguistics]`/`[Practice]`. This is called epenthesis when a vowel is inserted.

Both patterns are systematic linguistic transfer from L1 phonology, not random error — which is precisely why they are predictable, trainable, and worth dedicated practice rather than vague "practice more" advice.

### This is not the same issue as accent

Per Part I Ch.2, accent is systematic variation that remains intelligible; the two patterns above are phonemic substitutions and structural simplifications that can cross an intelligibility boundary — producing *think* as "sink," or *bag* as "ba," changes what a listener (or the scoring engine) perceives, which is exactly the kind of error the Pronunciation band descriptors penalize `[Pearson]` (see Part I Ch.1 and Ch.2).

## Examples

| Target word | IPA | Common Mandarin-L1 substitution pattern | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| think | /θɪŋk/ | "sink" /sɪŋk/ | /θ/ absent from Mandarin inventory → substituted with /s/ |
| this | /ðɪs/ | "dis" /dɪs/ | /ð/ absent from Mandarin inventory → substituted with /d/ |
| bag | /bæg/ | "ba" or "ba-guh" | final /g/ not a permitted Mandarin coda → deletion or epenthesis |
| asked | /æskt/ | "as-kuh" or "as" | consonant cluster /skt/ not permitted in Mandarin → epenthesis or deletion |

## Exam Strategy

- When practicing PTE vocabulary (Part III), pay deliberate attention to word-final consonants and consonant clusters — this is a higher-yield target for Mandarin L1 speakers than most other pronunciation issues, because it is systematic rather than occasional.
- Practice holding a final consonant without adding a vowel after it: say the consonant's articulation and stop, rather than releasing into an "uh" sound.
- For /θ/ and /ð/, practice the tongue-between-teeth articulation explicitly rather than substituting the nearest Mandarin sound — this is a place-of-articulation skill, not a matter of accent.

## Exercises

1. Record yourself saying five English words ending in a single consonant (e.g. *cat*, *dog*, *stop*). Listen for whether you added a vowel sound after the final consonant.
2. Record yourself saying five words with word-final consonant clusters (e.g. *asks*, *texts*, *helped*). Identify which consonants you may be dropping.
3. Practice a /θ/–/s/ minimal pair list (e.g. *think/sink*, *thick/sick*, *thumb/sum*) and self-check tongue placement (between the teeth for /θ/, behind the teeth for /s/).

## Summary

- English has 24 consonant phonemes, organized by place, manner, and voicing `[Linguistics]`.
- Mandarin's phoneme inventory lacks /θ/ and /ð/, producing predictable substitutions `[Linguistics]`.
- Mandarin's syllable structure only permits vowel, /n/, /ŋ/, or glide codas — no other final consonants and no consonant clusters — producing predictable deletion or epenthesis in English `[Linguistics]`.
- These are systematic transfer patterns from L1 phonology, not random mistakes, which is what makes them trainable.
- These errors affect intelligibility (and therefore Pronunciation score), unlike accent-level variation covered in Part I Ch.2.
