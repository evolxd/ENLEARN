# 9. Incomplete Plosion（不完全爆破）

Part: Part II — Pronunciation Foundations
Status: Draft
Accent model: General American (GA); this feature is shared broadly across English varieties.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Describe the three phases of a plosive consonant: closure, hold, release.
- Define incomplete plosion and identify the consonant environments where it typically occurs.
- Explain why incomplete plosion is a normal native-speech feature rather than an articulation error.
- Distinguish incomplete plosion from the Ch.2 pattern of Mandarin-L1 final-consonant deletion or epenthesis.

## Theory

### Key Terms 关键术语

| English | 中文 | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Plosive | 塞音 | A consonant produced by fully blocking airflow, then releasing it (e.g. /p b t d k g/). |
| Closure | 闭塞 | The phase where the articulators block airflow completely. |
| Release | 除阻 | The phase where the blocked airflow is released, producing an audible burst. |
| Incomplete Plosion | 不完全爆破 | A plosive's closure is formed but not audibly released before the next consonant begins. |

### The three phases of a plosive

English plosives — /p b t d k g/ — are produced in three phases: closure (the articulators fully block airflow), hold (the blockage is briefly maintained), and release (the blockage is released, producing an audible burst of air) `[Linguistics]`. In careful, isolated pronunciation, all three phases are audible.

### What happens when two consonants meet

When a plosive is immediately followed by another consonant — especially another plosive, but also certain other consonant types — native speakers typically perform the closure and hold, but do not audibly release the first consonant before moving directly into the articulation of the second `[Linguistics]`. This is called incomplete plosion (or an "unreleased stop"). For example, in *"good boy"*, the /d/ of *good* forms its closure but is not released before the lips close for the /b/ of *boy* — the release phase is essentially absorbed into the following consonant's articulation.

This happens for the same articulatory-ease reason introduced in Ch.6: releasing the first plosive fully and then producing the second consonant separately requires an extra articulatory step that fluent speech tends to skip when it serves no communicative purpose (the listener can still identify the first consonant from its closure and surrounding context).

### This is normal, not sloppy

Incomplete plosion is a systematic, expected feature of fluent native English, not a sign of careless speech `[Linguistics]`. A listener still perceives that the first consonant was produced — its closure, and the preceding vowel's shape, carry enough information — even without hearing its release. This matters directly for Mandarin L1 learners: over-articulating every plosive with a full, audible release before starting the next consonant is not "more correct" English; it is a marked deviation from the native pattern that this chapter documents.

### Distinguishing this from the Ch.2 error pattern

It is important not to conflate incomplete plosion with the Ch.2 pattern of Mandarin-L1 learners adding an extra vowel after a word-final consonant (e.g. *bag* → "ba-guh"). These are different phenomena with different causes:

- **Incomplete plosion** (this chapter): a native pattern where a plosive's release is dropped *before another consonant*, with no vowel inserted.
- **Ch.2's epenthesis pattern**: a Mandarin-L1 transfer pattern where an extra vowel is inserted *after* a final consonant, often because Mandarin's syllable structure does not permit that consonant as a coda on its own.

The practical implication is the opposite of what a learner might assume: rather than needing to add a fuller release to sound more careful, Mandarin L1 learners typically need to *practice not adding anything at all* after certain consonants — no release, and no extra vowel.

## Examples

| Phrase | What happens | IPA-style note |
|---|---|---|
| good boy | /d/ closure formed, not released before /b/ | /ɡʊd̚ bɔɪ/ (d̚ = unreleased) |
| big cat | /g/ closure formed, not released before /k/ | /bɪɡ̚ kæt/ |
| that time | /t/ closure formed, not released before /t/ | /ðæt̚ taɪm/ |

## Exam Strategy

- Do not insert an audible release (or worse, an extra vowel) between a word-final plosive and a following word that starts with a consonant — this is the opposite of the native pattern and can sound over-articulated, working against the "smooth rhythm and phrasing" Oral Fluency criterion (Part I Ch.3).
- Practice forming the closure for a final plosive and moving directly into the next consonant's articulation without a release burst or added vowel.
- This chapter's target applies specifically to plosive-plus-consonant sequences across (or within) word boundaries — it does not apply when the following sound is a vowel, where linking (Ch.8) applies instead.

## Exercises

1. Practice the three example phrases above, focusing on forming the first plosive's closure without releasing it before starting the second consonant.
2. Record yourself saying *"good boy"* two ways: with a full release of the /d/, and with incomplete plosion. Compare which sounds closer to natural native speech.
3. Identify a habit in your own speech: do you tend to add a small vowel sound after final plosives before a following consonant? If so, practice this chapter's target directly on that pattern.

## Summary

- Plosives have three phases: closure, hold, release; all three are audible only in careful, isolated speech `[Linguistics]`.
- When a plosive is followed directly by another consonant, native speakers typically skip the audible release — this is incomplete plosion `[Linguistics]`.
- Incomplete plosion is a normal, expected native-speech feature, not sloppy articulation.
- This is distinct from the Ch.2 Mandarin-L1 pattern of inserting an extra vowel after a final consonant — the two should not be conflated, and the practical training target (no release, no added vowel) is the same underlying skill either way.
