# 6. Connected Speech（连贯语流）

Part: Part II — Pronunciation Foundations
Status: Draft
Accent model: General American (GA)

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Define connected speech as the umbrella term covering how sounds change, link, or drop in continuous natural speech.
- Explain the two underlying causes of connected speech phenomena: rhythmic compression and articulatory ease.
- Distinguish the four main phenomena — reduction, linking, assimilation, elision — and identify which get dedicated chapters in this Part.
- Explain why recognizing connected speech is critical for listening-based PTE items, not only for speaking.

## Theory

### Key Terms 关键术语

| English | 中文 | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Connected Speech | 连贯语流 | The systematic changes sounds undergo when words are spoken in a continuous natural stream rather than in isolation. |
| Coarticulation | 协同发音 | The overlapping influence of adjacent speech sounds on each other during production. |
| Assimilation | 同化 | A sound changes to become more similar to a neighboring sound. |
| Elision | 省音／脱落 | A sound is dropped entirely in connected speech. |

### Why connected speech happens

Two mechanisms, both introduced in earlier chapters of this Part, jointly produce connected speech `[Linguistics]`:

1. **Rhythmic compression** (Ch.5): because English rhythm concentrates prominence on content words and compresses the material around them, unstressed function words and syllables are under pressure to shorten, reduce, and merge with their neighbors.
2. **Articulatory ease (coarticulation)**: adjacent sounds influence each other physically as the mouth transitions between them; producing two very different articulations back-to-back at speed is effortful, so speech tends to smooth those transitions `[Linguistics]`.

Neither mechanism is specific to English — coarticulation is a cross-linguistic property of spoken language — but the *specific patterns* it produces in English are what this Part documents, because they are what a listener (human or automated) expects to hear in natural speech, and what a PTE Repeat Sentence or Listening item may present to you in compressed form.

### The four main phenomena

This chapter is the overview; each phenomenon below either has its own dedicated chapter in this Part or is introduced here because it does not warrant one on its own for this book's PTE-focused scope.

| Phenomenon | Example | Covered in |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduction** — a function word's vowel weakens toward schwa | *"to"* → /tə/ | Ch.7 (own chapter) |
| **Linking** — a consonant or glide bridges two adjacent sounds across a word boundary | *"turn it off"* → /tɜːr nɪ tɒf/ | Ch.8 (own chapter) |
| **Assimilation** — a sound shifts to become more similar to its neighbor | *"handbag"* → /hæmbæg/ (n → m before b) | This chapter |
| **Elision** — a sound is dropped entirely | *"next day"* → /neks deɪ/ (t dropped) | This chapter |

(Incomplete Plosion, covered in Ch.9, is a related but distinct phenomenon specific to stop consonants before another consonant — it is given its own chapter due to its high relevance to PTE delivery, rather than folded into elision here.)

### Assimilation in more detail

Assimilation occurs when a sound changes its place or manner of articulation to become more similar to an adjacent sound, easing the transition between them `[Linguistics]`. A common pattern in English is place assimilation of alveolar consonants (/n/, /t/, /d/) before bilabial or velar consonants: *handbag* is often produced /hæmbæg/ (n → m before the bilabial b), and *good girl* may show a velar-influenced /d/. This is a normal, expected feature of fluent native speech, not an error `[Linguistics]`.

### Elision in more detail

Elision occurs when a sound — most often a consonant in a complex cluster, or an unstressed vowel — is dropped entirely from connected speech `[Linguistics]`. *Next day* is commonly produced /neks deɪ/, with the /t/ of *next* elided before the /d/ of *day*; *friendship* may reduce the middle /d/ in casual speech. This is distinct from the Mandarin-L1 final-consonant deletion pattern discussed in Ch.2: native elision is rule-governed and occurs in specific consonant-cluster environments, while the Ch.2 pattern is a phoneme-inventory transfer effect that can apply more broadly and unpredictably. Confusing the two is a risk: a learner should not treat "natives drop sounds too" as license to drop codas indiscriminately (Ch.2's pattern), since native elision follows narrower, specific rules.

### Why this matters beyond your own speech

Connected speech is not only a speaking concern. Repeat Sentence and Listening items present you with native or near-native speech containing exactly these compressions `[Pearson]`/`[Linguistics]` — if you have only ever practiced hearing words in their careful, isolated citation form, compressed connected speech can sound unfamiliar or even like different words entirely, which directly threatens Content accuracy on Repeat Sentence (Part I Ch.4) and comprehension on Listening items (Part V).

## Examples

*"I don't know what I want."* in careful citation speech vs. natural connected speech: /aɪ doʊnt noʊ wʌt aɪ wɒnt/ (careful) vs. something closer to /aɪ dəno wədaɪ wɒnt/ in fast natural speech (schwa reduction on *don't*/*what*, linking across word boundaries). Recognizing that these represent the *same* underlying sentence is a decoding skill, not just a production skill.

## Exam Strategy

- For Repeat Sentence: train your ear on connected-speech audio, not just careful/citation-form audio, so compressed forms do not sound like unfamiliar input during the exam.
- For your own speaking: apply reduction and linking (Ch.7–8) deliberately to function words and word boundaries, but do not extend elision to word-final content-word consonants — that crosses into the Ch.2 error pattern rather than authentic connected speech.
- Treat this chapter as a map: if a specific compressed form confuses you, identify which of the four phenomena it is, then study the relevant chapter (Ch.7 Reduction, Ch.8 Linking, or the assimilation/elision sections above) rather than treating it as a one-off exception.

## Exercises

1. Listen to a short piece of natural (not slowed-down) English audio and try to identify one example each of assimilation and elision, using the patterns described above.
2. Take the sentence *"handbag"*, *"good girl"*, and *"next day"* and practice both the careful and the naturally-assimilated/elided pronunciations, so you can recognize both forms.
3. Reflect: identify one place in your own speech where you might be applying elision too broadly (e.g. dropping a content-word final consonant) rather than following the narrower native pattern.

## Summary

- Connected speech covers the systematic ways sounds change, link, or drop in continuous natural speech, driven by rhythmic compression (Ch.5) and articulatory ease `[Linguistics]`.
- Four main phenomena: reduction (Ch.7), linking (Ch.8), assimilation, and elision — the latter two introduced in this chapter.
- Assimilation and elision are rule-governed, native-speech features, distinct from the Mandarin-L1 coda-deletion pattern covered in Ch.2.
- Recognizing connected speech is essential for Repeat Sentence and Listening comprehension, not only for your own delivery.
