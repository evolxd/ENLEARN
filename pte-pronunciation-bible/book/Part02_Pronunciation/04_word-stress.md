# 4. Word Stress（单词重音）

Part: Part II — Pronunciation Foundations
Status: Draft
Accent model: General American (GA); stress placement itself is largely shared with RP.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Define primary and secondary word stress and how they are marked in IPA.
- Explain that English word stress is lexical (must often be learned per word) rather than fully predictable, unlike some other languages.
- Identify stress-shift patterns caused by common derivational suffixes.
- Explain why Mandarin's lack of an English-style stress system predicts a specific, recurring error pattern.

## Theory

### Key Terms 关键术语

| English | 中文 | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Primary Stress | 主重音 | The most prominent syllable in a word, marked ˈ before it in IPA. |
| Secondary Stress | 次重音 | A syllable with less prominence than primary stress but more than an unstressed syllable, marked ˌ. |
| Lexical Stress | 词汇重音 | Stress that is a fixed property of a specific word, not predictable from a general rule alone. |
| Stress Shift | 重音转移 | A change in which syllable carries primary stress when a word's form changes (e.g. via a suffix). |
| Tone (lexical) | 声调 | In Mandarin, a pitch pattern that distinguishes word meaning — a different mechanism from English stress. |

### What stress actually is

English stress is realized through a combination of pitch, length, loudness, and vowel quality — the stressed syllable is typically higher-pitched, longer, louder, and has a full vowel, while unstressed syllables are shorter, quieter, and often reduced toward schwa `[Linguistics]` (Part II Ch.7 covers this reduction in depth). Primary stress is marked ˈ immediately before the stressed syllable in IPA; secondary stress, when present, is marked ˌ, e.g. /ˌɪntərˈnæʃənəl/ for *international*.

### Stress is lexical, not fully predictable

Unlike some languages with fixed, rule-based stress (e.g. French, which regularly stresses the final syllable), English stress placement must often be learned as a property of the individual word `[Linguistics]`. There are partial patterns tied to specific suffixes — for example, words ending in *-tion*, *-ic*, or *-ity* typically place primary stress on the syllable immediately before the suffix `[Linguistics]` — but these are tendencies for word families, not a single rule covering all of English vocabulary. This is why Part III (Lexicon) records stress as a required field for every word: it is not something a learner can reliably derive on demand.

### Stress shift within word families

A single root can carry primary stress on different syllables depending on which derivational suffix is attached `[Linguistics]`:

| Word | IPA | Stressed syllable |
|---|---|---|
| photograph | /ˈfoʊtəɡræf/ | 1st |
| photography | /fəˈtɑːɡrəfi/ | 2nd |
| photographic | /ˌfoʊtəˈɡræfɪk/ | 3rd |

Each shift follows from the suffix attached (*-y* and *-ic* pull stress to different syllables relative to the base word), which is why memorizing a word's stress in isolation is not enough — related word forms in Part III's "Related Words" field often need their own stress check.

### Why this is a predictable difficulty for Mandarin L1 speakers

Mandarin is a tonal language: pitch contour on each syllable (tone) distinguishes word meaning, but Mandarin does not have an English-style system of one stressed syllable standing out against unstressed neighbors within a word `[Linguistics]`. Because Mandarin syllables are not organized around this stressed/unstressed contrast, Mandarin L1 learners frequently give each syllable of a multisyllabic English word similar prominence, rather than concentrating stress on one syllable and reducing the others `[Linguistics]`/`[Practice]`. This connects directly to the Pronunciation band descriptors in Part I Ch.1, which explicitly require "stress placed correctly in all words" for the top band — this is a scored criterion independent of accent (Part I Ch.2).

## Examples

Misplaced stress is not merely an accent feature — it can obscure the word entirely. Compare **record** as a noun (/ˈrekərd/, stress on 1st syllable) versus as a verb (/rɪˈkɔːrd/, stress on 2nd syllable): these are two different words distinguished only by stress placement, and using the wrong one changes meaning, not just naturalness `[Linguistics]`.

## Exam Strategy

- For every new word studied in Part III, check and mark its stress pattern before attempting to shadow it — do not guess stress from spelling or from the stress pattern of a related word.
- For noun/verb pairs distinguished by stress (e.g. *record*, *present*, *object*), treat stress as carrying the same functional load as a phoneme contrast — getting it wrong can be a Content-level error, not just a Pronunciation-band reduction.
- Practice exaggerating the contrast between stressed and unstressed syllables during drilling (even beyond what feels natural at first), since the common Mandarin-L1 error is under-differentiating them, not over-differentiating them.

## Exercises

1. Take the word family *photograph / photography / photographic* (or a similar family from your own vocabulary list) and mark primary stress on each form. Say all three aloud, exaggerating the stress shift.
2. Find three English noun/verb pairs distinguished by stress (e.g. *record*, *present*, *permit*) and practice both forms, noting the meaning change.
3. Record yourself saying a four-syllable word. Check whether all four syllables sound similarly prominent, or whether one clearly stands out — compare against the word's dictionary stress marking.

## Summary

- Stress is realized through pitch, length, loudness, and vowel quality, and is marked with ˈ (primary) and ˌ (secondary) in IPA `[Linguistics]`.
- English stress placement is lexical — it must often be learned per word, with only partial suffix-based patterns to rely on `[Linguistics]`.
- Stress can shift across a word family depending on the suffix attached, so related word forms need independent stress checks.
- Mandarin's tonal system does not produce an English-style stressed/unstressed contrast, making even-prominence errors a predictable pattern for Mandarin L1 speakers `[Linguistics]`.
- Correct stress is an explicitly scored PTE Pronunciation criterion, independent of accent (Part I Ch.1–2).
