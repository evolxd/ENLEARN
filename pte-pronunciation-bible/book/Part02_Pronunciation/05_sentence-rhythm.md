# 5. Sentence Rhythm（句子节奏）

Part: Part II — Pronunciation Foundations
Status: Draft
Accent model: General American (GA); the stress-timed rhythmic pattern is shared broadly across English varieties.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Explain the "stress-timed" model of English sentence rhythm and its limits as a description, not an absolute acoustic law.
- Distinguish content words from function words and state which typically carry sentence-level prominence.
- Explain why Mandarin's more syllable-timed rhythm predicts a specific, recurring naturalness problem for Mandarin L1 learners.
- Connect sentence rhythm to the Oral Fluency criteria introduced in Part I Ch.3.

## Theory

### Key Terms 关键术语

| English | 中文 | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Stress-Timing | 重音节拍 | A rhythmic pattern where stressed syllables tend to recur at roughly regular intervals, regardless of how many unstressed syllables lie between them. |
| Syllable-Timing | 音节节拍 | A rhythmic pattern where syllables tend to take up roughly similar amounts of time, regardless of stress. |
| Isochrony | 等时性 | Perceived regularity of timing between prominent beats in speech. |
| Content Word | 实词 | A word carrying core meaning: nouns, main verbs, adjectives, most adverbs, question words. |
| Function Word | 虚词 | A grammatical word: articles, prepositions, auxiliary verbs, pronouns, conjunctions. |
| Prominence | 突显度 | The degree to which a syllable or word stands out perceptually in connected speech. |

### English as a stress-timed language

English is commonly described as a "stress-timed" language: stressed syllables tend to recur at roughly regular intervals, and the unstressed syllables between them are compressed or stretched to fit that timing, rather than each syllable taking a fixed amount of time `[Linguistics]`. This is a widely used pedagogical model in English pronunciation teaching, but it should be stated carefully: acoustic research has shown that strict, measurable isochrony (perfectly equal time intervals) does not hold up as a literal physical fact in recorded speech `[Linguistics]` — it is better understood as a strong perceptual tendency and a useful teaching approximation than an exact acoustic law. This book uses it in that spirit: as a practical model for training rhythm, not a claim about millisecond-level timing.

### Content words vs function words

Within this rhythmic pattern, content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, most adverbs, wh-question words) typically carry the sentence's prominent beats, while function words (articles, prepositions, auxiliary verbs, pronouns, conjunctions) typically compress and reduce between them `[Linguistics]`. This directly connects to Ch.7 (Reduction): function words are exactly the words that commonly take a reduced, schwa-based weak form in natural speech.

### Mandarin's rhythmic pattern and what it predicts

Mandarin is often described as leaning more toward syllable-timing, where syllables tend to receive more comparable duration and prominence than in a stress-timed language `[Linguistics]` (this is a relative tendency across languages, not a strict binary category — some phoneticians treat the stress-timed/syllable-timed distinction itself as a spectrum rather than two discrete classes `[Linguistics]`). Because Mandarin does not train speakers to compress unstressed syllables the way English rhythm requires, Mandarin L1 learners speaking English frequently give each syllable relatively similar weight and duration `[Linguistics]`/`[Practice]`. The result is English that sounds evenly hammered out syllable-by-syllable rather than grouped into the rise-and-fall pattern native rhythm produces — this is a primary, mechanism-level explanation for why Mandarin-accented English is often perceived as sounding "choppy," independent of whether individual phonemes are correct.

### Why this matters for PTE specifically

Part I Ch.3 established that Oral Fluency rewards "smooth rhythm and phrasing," not raw speed, and penalizes staccato, disjointed delivery. Sentence rhythm — specifically, compressing function words and letting content words carry prominence — is the underlying mechanism that produces the smooth phrasing the band descriptors describe `[Pearson]`/`[Linguistics]`. Chapters 6–9 of this Part (Connected Speech, Reduction, Linking, Incomplete Plosion) are, collectively, the toolkit for producing this rhythm; this chapter explains why that toolkit exists.

## Examples

*"The MANager will MEET the CLIENTS at the AIRport."*

Capitalized syllables mark likely sentence-level prominence: content words (*manager*, *meet*, *clients*, *airport*) carry stress, while function words (*the*, *will*, *at*) compress between them. Read aloud with deliberately equal weight on every syllable, the same sentence sounds mechanical; read with content-word prominence and function-word compression, it approximates natural English rhythm.

## Exam Strategy

- Before a Read Aloud or Repeat Sentence response, quickly identify the content words in the sentence — these are your prominence targets — and plan to compress the function words around them rather than stressing every word evenly.
- Do not equate "clear pronunciation" with "equal emphasis on every word" — over-stressing function words is a common overcorrection that itself sounds unnatural and can work against smooth phrasing.
- Practice this chapter's rhythm pattern together with Ch.7's reduced forms — they are two views of the same underlying mechanism.

## Exercises

1. Take a sentence from a Read Aloud practice item. Underline the content words, then read the sentence aloud twice: once giving every syllable equal weight, once giving prominence only to the underlined words. Compare how each sounds against Part I Ch.3's Band 5 description.
2. Record a short spontaneous response (e.g. to a Describe Image prompt) and mark, on the transcript, which words you audibly stressed. Are they mostly content words, or is stress scattered evenly?
3. Practice reading a sentence at a deliberately exaggerated stress-timed rhythm (strongly compressing function words) — this is a useful drilling exaggeration, not necessarily the target performance, similar to how Ch.4 recommended exaggerating stress contrast during practice.

## Summary

- English rhythm is commonly modeled as stress-timed: stressed syllables recur at roughly regular perceptual intervals, though this is a pedagogical approximation rather than a strict acoustic law `[Linguistics]`.
- Content words typically carry sentence-level prominence; function words typically compress and reduce between them `[Linguistics]`.
- Mandarin's more syllable-timed tendency predicts that Mandarin L1 speakers will often give English syllables overly even weight, producing choppy-sounding rhythm independent of phoneme accuracy `[Linguistics]`.
- This chapter's rhythm model is the mechanism underlying Part I Ch.3's Oral Fluency criteria, and sets up Chapters 6–9 of this Part as its practical toolkit.
