# 3. Describe Image（看图说话）

Part: Part V — Question Strategy
Status: Draft
Accent model: General American (GA), consistent with Parts II and IV

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- State Describe Image's format and its key-point-based Content mechanism, extending Part I Ch.4.
- Explain why generic memorized templates carry documented risk under current scoring practices.
- Use preparation time to maximize distinct relevant key points rather than elaborating narrowly.
- Apply Part II's rhythm and reduction tools to a spontaneous (non-scripted) response.

## Theory

### Key Terms 关键术语

This chapter reuses "key-point matching" from Part I Ch.4 rather than redefining it.

### Format and scoring recap

Describe Image shows an image (chart, graph, map, diagram, or picture) with a preparation window (commonly reported around 25 seconds) followed by a response window (commonly reported around 40 seconds) `[Practice]` (secondary-sourced; re-verify before publication). Content, Oral Fluency, and Pronunciation are scored using the models established in Part I Ch.1 and Ch.4; Content specifically uses the key-point/relevance matching mechanism (Part I Ch.4) rather than exact-text matching, since there is no single fixed script for describing an image.

### A documented shift toward combined AI and human content review

Multiple test-prep sources report that Pearson has moved Describe Image (and Retell Lecture, Part V Ch.4) toward a combined AI-and-human review process for Content specifically, intended to better evaluate genuine communication of information and to flag generic, templated, or clearly pre-prepared responses `[Practice]` — this is reported via secondary test-prep sources rather than a directly-fetched primary Pearson document, and should be re-verified before publication. If accurate, this has a direct strategic implication: a response that closely follows a memorized, generic template (rather than genuinely describing the specific image shown) carries a documented risk of being flagged and scored down on Content, independent of how fluent or well-pronounced the delivery is.

### Using preparation time for key-point breadth

Building directly on Part I Ch.4's finding for open-response items: within a 40-second response window, stating several distinct, genuinely relevant facts about the image typically covers more scoring ground under a key-point mechanism than elaborating at length on a single feature. Preparation time is best spent identifying 4–6 distinct, specific observations (a trend, a highest/lowest value, a notable comparison, a label or category, a general purpose) rather than planning one polished sentence.

## Examples

For a bar chart showing quarterly sales: an effective preparation-time list might include (1) overall trend across quarters, (2) the highest quarter and its approximate value, (3) the lowest quarter, (4) the size of the gap between highest and lowest, (5) any notable single-quarter change. A response addressing four or five of these points, even briefly, is likely to cover more of the expected key-point set than a longer, single-point elaboration — consistent with Part I Ch.4's Describe Image example.

## Exam Strategy

- Avoid rehearsing and reusing a fixed generic template across different images — describe the specific data or content actually shown, given the documented risk of templated-response detection.
- Use preparation time to list distinct observations, not to draft a polished single narrative.
- Apply Part II's content-word prominence and function-word reduction (Ch.5, Ch.7) even in spontaneous, unscripted speech — these mechanisms are not specific to read-aloud text.
- If you run out of new points before the response window ends, briefly restate the overall trend rather than going silent — a coherent close is preferable to an abrupt stop.

## Exercises

1. Practice with three different image types (bar chart, line graph, process diagram) and generate a 4–6 point preparation list for each within 25 seconds.
2. Record a Describe Image response and check afterward whether it followed a generic structure you could have used for a different image, or whether it was specific to the image shown.
3. Apply Part II Ch.5's content-word/function-word distinction to a recorded spontaneous response, checking for natural rhythm despite the lack of a fixed script.

## Summary

- Describe Image uses key-point/relevance Content scoring (Part I Ch.4), not exact-text matching.
- Reported scoring practice has moved toward combined AI-and-human Content review that can flag generic templated responses — a specific, documented risk to relying on memorized templates `[Practice]`.
- Preparation time is most effective when used to list distinct relevant observations rather than to draft one elaborated point.
- Part II's rhythm and reduction mechanisms apply to spontaneous speech exactly as they do to read text.
