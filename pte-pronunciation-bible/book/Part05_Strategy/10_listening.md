# 10. Listening（听力）

### Format and item types

The Listening section is commonly reported to last around 30–43 minutes across roughly eight item types `[Practice]` (secondary-sourced; re-verify before publication), including:

- **Summarize Spoken Text**: hear a lecture (commonly reported 60–90 seconds), then write a 50–70 word summary within a 10-minute window; scored on content, form, and language quality, contributing to both Listening and Writing `[Practice]`.
- **Fill in the Blanks**: a transcript with blanks, completed by listening to the audio.
- **Highlight Correct Summary**: select the paragraph that best summarizes the audio.
- **Highlight Incorrect Words**: read a text while listening to its audio version, which contains altered words; identify each alteration.
- **Write from Dictation**: hear a short sentence (commonly reported 3–5 seconds) and type it verbatim.
- Additional reported item types include Multiple Choice (single/multiple answer) and Select Missing Word.

Scoring varies by item type: some are simple correct/incorrect (one point, no negative marking); Multiple Choice (Multiple Answer) and Highlight Incorrect Words are reported to apply negative marking (a point deducted per incorrect selection, alongside points earned for correct ones) `[Practice]`; Summarize Spoken Text uses a more detailed multi-dimension scoring model similar to the writing items in this Part.

### Why Write from Dictation and Highlight Incorrect Words test Part II directly

Both item types require distinguishing the exact words actually spoken from what a listener might otherwise expect or mishear — and spoken PTE audio is not slowed-down, citation-form speech `[Pearson]`/`[Linguistics]` (Part II Ch.6). A learner who has only trained their ear on careful, separated-word audio is at a specific, documented-mechanism disadvantage here: weak forms (Ch.7), linked word boundaries (Ch.8), and assimilated/elided sounds (Ch.6) are all things Write from Dictation requires you to correctly resolve back into their full written form, and Highlight Incorrect Words requires you to notice precisely where the spoken version diverges from the printed text — which is much harder if you cannot reliably distinguish a genuine word substitution from an expected connected-speech reduction.

### Applying Part II in reverse

Part II's chapters were framed around production (how to speak this way). For Listening, the same knowledge applies in reverse, as a recognition tool: knowing that *"want to"* is expected to reduce to something close to /ˈwɒnə/ (Part II Ch.7) means a learner will not mistake this for an unfamiliar word or a dictation error — they will correctly transcribe it as "want to." This reversal is why this book places its Listening chapter last: it draws on nearly every mechanism introduced earlier in the book, applied to comprehension instead of production.

## Examples

Write from Dictation audio: */aɪ dəno wədaɪ wɒnt/* (natural connected speech for *"I don't know what I want"*). A listener trained on Part II Ch.6–7 recognizes the reduced *don't know* and *what I* as expected patterns and transcribes the full, correctly-spelled sentence; a listener without this training risks mishearing individual sounds as unfamiliar or omitting words that were genuinely present but reduced.

## Exam Strategy

- Train listening comprehension specifically on natural-speed, connected-speech audio, not only slowed or citation-form audio, mirroring the same principle established for Repeat Sentence (Part V Ch.2).
- For Write from Dictation, write the full, standard-spelled words you infer were intended, even if the audio realization was reduced or linked — the scoring target is the standard written form, not a phonetic transcription of what you heard.
- For Highlight Incorrect Words, focus attention on content words (which carry the informational load) while remembering that function-word reductions are expected and not necessarily "incorrect" changes.
- Be aware of which item types apply negative marking (Multiple Choice Multiple Answer, Highlight Incorrect Words) and avoid guessing indiscriminately on those specifically, unlike no-penalty item types where an educated guess has no downside.

## Exercises

1. Practice Write from Dictation using natural-speed audio and check errors specifically against Part II's reduction (Ch.7) and linking (Ch.8) patterns — are your errors "mishearing" or "not recognizing an expected pattern"?
2. For a Highlight Incorrect Words passage, listen once for content-word changes only, then a second time checking function words, and compare accuracy between the two passes.
3. Review which Listening item types you have practiced apply negative marking, and adjust your guessing strategy for those items specifically.
