# 1. Read Aloud（朗读）

Part: Part V — Question Strategy
Status: Draft
Accent model: General American (GA), consistent with Parts II and IV

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- State Read Aloud's format and the three enabling skills it is scored on, building directly on Part I Ch.1.
- Use the preparation window to scan a passage for the specific high-risk patterns documented in Part IV.
- Apply Part II's reduction, linking, and stress mechanisms while reading, rather than reading word-by-word.
- Recover from a mid-passage error without restarting, per Part I Ch.1's fluency-weighting finding.

## Theory

### Key Terms 关键术语

| English | 中文 | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Preview | 预览 | Silently scanning the passage during preparation time before reading aloud. |
| Chunking | 分块／意群划分 | Grouping words into meaning units before speaking (Part II Ch.5). |
| High-Risk Word | 高风险词 | A word likely to trigger one of the documented error patterns from Part IV. |

### Format and scoring recap

Read Aloud presents a written passage, commonly reported at around 60–80 words, with a preparation window (commonly reported around 35 seconds, scaling somewhat with passage length) before the microphone opens for a single response window (commonly reported around 30–40 seconds) `[Practice]` — these figures come from secondary test-prep sources rather than a directly-fetched primary Pearson document (see Part I's sourcing note) and should be re-verified against the current official test format before publication. Scoring follows the model established in Part I Ch.1: Content (exact-text word-matching), Oral Fluency, and Pronunciation, with Oral Fluency and Pronunciation combined typically outweighing Content `[Practice]`.

### The preparation window is a scanning tool, not a memorization task

Given that outweighing (Part I Ch.1) and the specific error patterns documented in Part IV, the highest-value use of preparation time is not attempting to memorize the passage, but scanning it for a short, specific checklist:

1. **Chunk boundaries** (Part II Ch.5): where do the natural meaning-group breaks fall?
2. **High-risk phonemes** (Part IV Ch.1): does the passage contain /θ/, /ð/, /v/, or words with final consonants/clusters?
3. **Stress-risk words** (Part IV Ch.2): are there multisyllabic words or noun/verb stress pairs (e.g. *record*, *present*) whose stress you should confirm before speaking?
4. **Linking points** (Part II Ch.8): where does a consonant-final word meet a vowel-initial word?

This checklist converts a generic "prepare the passage" instruction into the same mechanism-based practice this book has built toward since Part I.

### Reading with reduction, linking, and stress — not word-by-word

Applying Part II Ch.5–8 directly: content words should carry clear stress and full vowel quality; function words should reduce to their weak forms (Ch.7); word boundaries identified in the checklist above should link (Ch.8) rather than pause. A passage read with each word given equal, separated weight will sound closer to Part I Ch.3's Band 1–2 descriptors than Band 4–5, even if every word is technically pronounced "correctly" in isolation — because Oral Fluency measures the *pattern* of delivery, not just individual-word accuracy.

### Handling an error without restarting

Part I Ch.1 already established the core rule: a restart introduces a fluency disruption (repetition/false start) on top of whatever the original error cost. For Read Aloud specifically, this means: if you misread a word, keep going. Do not stop, and do not repeat the sentence or phrase to "fix" it.

## Examples

Sample passage excerpt: *"The research team analyzed data from over three thousand participants to determine the long-term effects of the treatment."*

Preview checklist applied:
- Chunk boundaries: *"The research team"* / *"analyzed data from over three thousand participants"* / *"to determine the long-term effects of the treatment"*
- High-risk phonemes: *"three"* (/θ/), *"thousand"* (/θ/), *"effects"* (final consonant cluster /kts/)
- Stress-risk: *"research"* (noun/verb stress pair — as a noun here, primary stress on the first syllable in GA), *"determine"* (stress on the second syllable)
- Linking points: *"team analyzed"*, *"data from"*, *"over three"*, *"effects of"*

## Exam Strategy

- Run the four-item checklist above during preparation time as a matter of habit — this converts unstructured "reading practice" into a checklist matched directly to what is actually scored (Part I Ch.1).
- Prioritize maintaining chunked, linked delivery over pausing to get an individual difficult word exactly right — the fluency cost of stopping usually exceeds the content cost of one imperfect word.
- After each practice attempt, check your recording specifically against the four checklist items, not just "did it sound okay."

## Exercises

1. Take a Read Aloud practice passage and apply the four-item checklist in writing before recording yourself.
2. Record the same passage twice: once reading each word with equal separation, once applying chunking, reduction, and linking. Compare both against Part I Ch.3's Band descriptors.
3. Deliberately misread one word in a practice passage and continue to the end without stopping. Notice how this differs from your instinct to restart.

## Summary

- Read Aloud scoring follows the Part I Ch.1 model: Content via exact-text matching, weighted alongside Oral Fluency and Pronunciation, which together typically carry more weight.
- Preparation time is best used as a structured scan for chunk boundaries, high-risk phonemes, stress-risk words, and linking points — not memorization.
- Applying Part II's reduction, linking, and stress mechanisms during reading is what separates Band 4–5 delivery from technically-correct-but-choppy delivery.
- Never restart after an error; continuing avoids stacking a fluency disruption on top of the original mistake.
