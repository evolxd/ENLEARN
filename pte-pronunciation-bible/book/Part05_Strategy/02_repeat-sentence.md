# 2. Repeat Sentence（复述句子）

Part: Part V — Question Strategy
Status: Draft
Accent model: General American (GA), consistent with Parts II and IV

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- State Repeat Sentence's format and partial-credit Content scoring model.
- Explain why this item tests connected-speech decoding (Part II Ch.6), not just memory.
- Apply chunked listening instead of word-for-word memorization for longer sentences.
- Reproduce a heard sentence using natural connected speech rather than a robotic word-by-word rendition.

## Theory

### Key Terms 关键术语

| English | 中文 | Definition |

| Partial Credit | 部分给分 | A scoring model where a response can earn some points without being fully correct. |

### Format and scoring recap

Repeat Sentence plays a short audio sentence, commonly reported at roughly 3–9 seconds, after a brief (commonly reported ~3 second) preparation window, with a further response window (commonly reported around 15 seconds) to repeat it `[Practice]` (secondary-sourced; re-verify against primary format before publication). Content scoring uses partial credit rather than an all-or-nothing model: reproducing the full sentence earns full Content credit, a roughly half-correct reproduction earns partial credit, and a largely incorrect or missing response earns minimal credit `[Practice]`. Pronunciation and Oral Fluency are scored using the same band-descriptor model introduced in Part I Ch.1.

### Why this item is a listening-decoding test, not just a memory test

Repeat Sentence audio is typically produced as natural connected speech, not slow, isolated word-by-word citation-form speech `[Pearson]`/`[Linguistics]` (Part II Ch.6). This means the sentence you hear may already contain reduced function words (Part II Ch.7), linked word boundaries (Ch.8), and assimilated or elided sounds (Ch.6). A learner who has only practiced recognizing careful, citation-form English may perceive the compressed audio as unfamiliar or may mishear individual words — this is a decoding failure, not a memory failure, and it is exactly the risk Part II Ch.6 flagged when it said connected speech is "not only a speaking concern."

### Chunked listening instead of word-for-word memorization

For short sentences, word-for-word memorization may be feasible. For longer sentences, attempting to hold every individual word in memory is less reliable than processing the sentence the way native listeners do: in meaning chunks (Part II Ch.5), then reconstructing the sentence from those chunks rather than from a word list. This also naturally supports better Oral Fluency, since chunk-based reproduction tends to preserve natural phrasing rather than producing a hesitant, word-by-word recitation.

### Reproduce with natural delivery, not robotic word-by-word repetition

Because Content is scored on whether the correct words were produced (Part I Ch.1), and Oral Fluency/Pronunciation are scored on delivery quality independently, there is no scoring benefit to reproducing the sentence in an unnaturally slow, separated way "to be safe." Applying Part II's reduction and linking to your reproduction (as long as the correct words are present) supports Content, Oral Fluency, and Pronunciation simultaneously — this is the same principle established in Part V Ch.1 for Read Aloud, applied here to reconstructed rather than read text.

## Examples

Heard audio (natural connected speech): *"I don't know when they're going to announce the results."* — likely realized close to /aɪ doʊnə noʊ wen ðɛr ɡoʊɪŋ tə əˈnaʊns ðə rɪˈzʌlts/ in natural speech (reduced *don't know*, weak *to*, linking throughout).

A learner trained only on careful citation-form audio may mishear *"I don't know"* as an unfamiliar sound string. A learner who has studied Part II Ch.6–8 recognizes this as the expected connected-speech realization of a sentence they already know at the word level.

## Exam Strategy

- Train your listening on natural-speed, connected-speech audio specifically for this item type, not only on slow, careful audio — the exam will not present the compressed forms as an exception.
- For sentences beyond about 6–8 words, consciously chunk what you hear into 2–3 meaning groups rather than trying to hold every word individually.
- Reproduce using your own natural weak forms and linking (Part II Ch.7–8) rather than an artificially careful, separated delivery — this supports Oral Fluency and Pronunciation without costing Content.
- If you miss a word or two, still attempt the full sentence structure — partial credit rewards a largely-correct reconstruction over silence or an incomplete fragment.

## Exercises

1. Listen to five natural-speed sentences (not slowed-down audio) and write down which words you initially misheard, then check whether they correspond to a reduction or linking pattern from Part II.
2. Practice chunked reconstruction: listen to an 8–10 word sentence once, and reproduce it by first recalling its 2–3 meaning chunks, then filling in each chunk's exact words.
3. Record yourself reproducing a sentence twice: once word-by-word and separated, once with natural reduction and linking. Compare both for Oral Fluency using Part I Ch.3's Band descriptors.

## Summary

- Repeat Sentence uses partial-credit Content scoring alongside the same Oral Fluency/Pronunciation model from Part I Ch.1.
- The audio uses natural connected speech, making this fundamentally a decoding test of Part II Ch.6's material, not only a memory test.
- Chunked listening is more reliable than word-for-word memorization for longer sentences.
- Reproducing with natural reduction and linking supports all three scored skills at once — there is no benefit to an artificially careful, separated delivery.
